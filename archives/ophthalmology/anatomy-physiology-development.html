Anatomy of Eye

Eye: Organ of sight.

Eyeball: It is a cystic structure kept distended by the pressure inside it.

  • Shape: Oblate spheroid
  • Poles: The central point on the maximal convexities on the anterior and posterior curvatures of the eyeball is called the anterior and posterior pole respectively.
  • Equator: Equator of eye lies at the midplane between two poles.
  • The bony orbit + fat → protect eyeball from injury.
Poles and Equators of the Eyeball

Coats of Eyeball

Consists of 3 coats:

  1. Outer fibrous layer / coat
  2. Middle vascular layer / coat
  3. Inner nervous layer / coat

i. Outer Fibrous Layer / Coat

Dense strong wall which protects the intraocular contents.

  • a. Anterior 1/6th of the fibrous coat is transparent → Cornea:
    • Transparent
    • Ellipsoid anterior
    • Constitutes 1/6th of eyeball
    • Main refracting surface of eye
    • Dioptric power: 43 – 45 diopter
  • b. Posterior 5/6th of the fibrous coat is opaque → Sclera:
    • Cornea is set into sclera like watch glass.
    • Junction between cornea and sclera: Limbus.
    • Conjunctiva is firmly attached at the limbus.

ii. Middle Vascular Coat (Uveal Tissue)

Consists of three parts, from anterior to posterior: iris, ciliary body, and choroid.

Function: Supplies nutrition to various structures of the eyeball.

  • Iris: Colored, free, circular diaphragm with an aperture in the center (pupil). Consists of endothelium, stroma, pigment cells, and two groups of plain muscle fibers: circular (sphincter pupillae) and radiating (dilator pupillae).
  • Ciliary Body: Triangular shape with base forward. Iris is attached to middle of its base. Consists of non-striated muscle fibers (ciliary muscles), stroma, and secretory epithelial cells. Consists of two main parts: pars plicata and pars plana.
  • Choroid: Dark brown, highly vascular layer situated between sclera and retina. Extends from ora serrata up to the aperture of optic nerve.

iii. Inner Nervous Layer / Coat

Retina: Concerned with visual sensation and projects to visual cortex through the visual pathway.

  • Composed of ten layers of different cells lying on pigmented epithelial layer.
  • Lines 3/4 of the eyeball from inside.
  • Has yellow spot i.e. Macula lutea: Most sensitive part of retina.

Segments and Chambers of Eyeball

Divided into two segments:

1. Anterior Segment

Consists of crystalline lens and structures anterior to it viz., iris, cornea, and two chambers filled with aqueous humor (anterior chamber and posterior chamber).

Gross Anatomy of the Eyeball

a. Anterior Chamber

  • Boundary:
    • Anteriorly: Back of cornea
    • Posteriorly: Anterior surface of iris and part of ciliary body
  • Dimensions:
    • Depth: 2.5 mm in the center in normal adults (shallower in hypermetropes, deeper in myopes; almost equal in two eyes of same individual).
    • Contains: 0.25 ml of aqueous humor.
    • Communicates with posterior chamber through the pupil.

b. Posterior Chamber

Triangular space.

  • Boundary:
    • Anteriorly: Posterior surface of iris and part of ciliary body
    • Posteriorly: Crystalline lens and its zonules
    • Laterally: Ciliary body

2. Posterior Segment

Consists of structures posterior to lens viz., vitreous humor (a gel-like material filling space behind lens), retina, choroid, and optic disc.

Lens

  • Transparent, circular, biconvex structure lying immediately behind pupil.
  • Suspended from ciliary body by the suspensory ligament (zonules of Zinn).
  • Enclosed within a transparent capsule.

Accessory Structures of the Eye

The eye is supported by several accessory structures (appendages):

  • Eyebrows: Arched ridges (2 in number) of supraorbital margin of frontal bone with hair projecting obliquely to protect eyeball from sweat, dust, and foreign bodies.
  • Eyelids and Eyelashes: Movable folds of tissue situated above and below front of each eye with eyelashes on free edges.
  • Lacrimal Apparatus: Consists of lacrimal gland and its ducts, accessory lacrimal glands, lacrimal canaliculi, lacrimal sac, and nasolacrimal duct.
  • Extraocular Muscles: 6 extrinsic muscles (4 recti and 2 oblique muscles) attached to eyeball and orbital wall. Voluntary control for looking in specific directions; autonomic control for convergence and accommodation.

Blood Supply of the Eye

A. Arterial Supply

Main source is ophthalmic artery, a branch of internal carotid artery.

  • Branches supplying eyeballs:
    1. Short posterior ciliary arteries (about 20 in number)
    2. Long posterior ciliary arteries (2 in number)
    3. Central retinal artery

B. Venous Drainage

  • Veins draining blood from eyeball:
    • Central retinal vein (drains blood from retina)
    • Anterior ciliary veins, short posterior ciliary veins, and 4 venae vorticosae (vortex veins) drain uveal tissue
  • Main venous channels receiving tributaries:
    • Superior ophthalmic vein
    • Inferior ophthalmic vein
    • Middle ophthalmic vein
    • Medial ophthalmic vein
    • Angular vein
    • Cavernous sinus
Blood Supply Schema of the Eye

Nerve Supply to the Eye

A. Sensory Supply

Ophthalmic Nerve: Smallest division of trigeminal (5th cranial) nerve. Supplies structures via 3 main branches:

  1. Lacrimal nerve: Lies in lateral part of orbit; supplies lacrimal gland, conjunctiva, and lateral part of upper eyelid.
  2. Frontal nerve: Divides into two branches:
    • Supratrochlear nerve: Supplies conjunctiva, middle part of upper eyelid, skin of forehead above nose root.
    • Supraorbital nerve: Supplies conjunctiva, central part of upper eyelid, skin of forehead and scalp.
  3. Nasociliary nerve: Branches include:
    • Long ciliary nerves (2 in number): Pierce sclera near optic nerve → run between sclera and choroid → supply ciliary body, iris, and cornea.
    • Communicating branches to ciliary ganglion: Sensory root; fibers pass along short ciliary nerve to supply ciliary body, iris, and cornea.
    • Posterior ethmoidal nerve: Supplies ethmoidal and sphenoidal sinuses.
    • Anterior ethmoidal nerve: Terminal branch; leaves orbit via anterior ethmoidal foramen.
    • Infratrochlear nerve: Terminal branch; supplies conjunctiva, lacrimal sac, caruncle, and medial eyelids.

B. Motor Supply

  • 3rd Cranial Nerve (Oculomotor):
    • Superior division: Levator palpebrae superioris, Superior rectus.
    • Inferior division: Medial rectus, Inferior rectus, Inferior oblique (also branch to ciliary ganglion).
  • 4th Cranial Nerve (Trochlear): Superior oblique muscle.
  • 6th Cranial Nerve (Abducens): Lateral rectus muscle.
  • 7th Cranial Nerve (Facial): Orbicularis oculi muscle.
Ciliary Ganglion Notes:

Peripheral parasympathetic ganglion placed in the course of oculomotor nerve near apex of orbit.

  • Sensory Root: Comes from nasociliary nerve.
  • Sympathetic Root: Comes from internal carotid plexus (does not relay here, passes via short ciliary nerves to blood vessels).
  • Parasympathetic Root: Arises from nerve to inferior oblique muscle; carries preganglionic fibers from Edinger-Westphal nucleus which relay here to supply sphincter pupillae and ciliary muscle.

C. Autonomic Supply

1. Parasympathetic Supply

  • Edinger-Westphal nucleus (midbrain): Preganglionic fibers via 3rd CN → Ciliary ganglion → Postganglionic fibers via short ciliary nerves → Ciliary muscle & Sphincter pupillae.
  • Salivatory nucleus (pons): Preganglionic fibers via 7th CN → Sphenopalatine ganglion → Postganglionic secretomotor fibers via Lacrimal nerve → Lacrimal gland.

2. Sympathetic Supply

Ciliospinal center of Budge (located in intermediolateral cell column of spinal cord at C8, T1, and T2 levels) → Preganglionic fibers → Cervical sympathetic chain → Superior cervical ganglion → Postganglionic fibers enter skull with internal carotid plexus to supply:

  • Orbital arteries (vasomotor via ophthalmic artery plexus)
  • Dilator pupillae muscle (via Gasserian ganglion / long ciliary nerves)
  • Blood vessels inside the eyeball (via short ciliary nerves)
  • Müller's muscle of eyelid (via cavernous plexus & 3rd CN branches)

Lymphatic Drainage

Lymphatics draining eyelids, conjunctiva, and orbital tissues are arranged in two groups:

  • Medial group: Drains into submandibular lymph nodes.
  • Lateral group: Drains into preauricular / sub-auricular lymph nodes.

Embryology of Eye

Development commences around day 22 (embryo has 8 pairs of somites, length ~2 mm).

Primordia Origin

  • Optic vesicle: Outgrowth of prosencephalon (neuroectodermal).
  • Lens placode: Specialized area of surface ectoderm.
  • Mesenchyme surrounding optic vesicle.
  • Visceral mesoderm of maxillary process.

Developmental Overview

Structures Developmental Process
Optic vesicle and Optic stalk Linear thickened area on prosencephalic neural plate forms optic sulcus → bulges out as optic vesicle. Proximal part constricts and elongates into optic stalk.
Lens placode and Lens vesicle Optic vesicle contacts surface ectoderm → ectoderm thickens to form lens placode → sinks below surface to form lens vesicle → separates from surface ectoderm at 33rd day of gestation.
Optic cup Optic vesicle converts into double-layered optic cup due to invagination by developing lens and differential wall growth. Margins enclose lens.

Development of Optic Nerve

Framework Development
Optic nerve fibers Develop from nerve fiber layer of retina; grow into optic stalk passing through choroidal fissure (by 6th week of gestation) posteriorly to brain.
Glial system Develops from neuroectodermal cells forming outer wall of optic stalk. Glial septa composed from inner wall.
Sheath of Optic Nerve Developed from layers of mesenchyme surrounding optic nerve (like meninges of CNS).
Myelination Takes place from brain distally; reaches lamina cribrosa just before birth and stops. (Extension beyond disc appears postnatally as congenital opaque nerve fibers).

Development of Crystalline Lens [KU 2003, 2008]

Develops from surface ectoderm under the following stages:

1. Primary Lens Fibers

Cells of posterior wall of lens vesicle elongate rapidly to form primary lens fibers, obliterating cavity of lens vesicle. Preserved up to 3rd month of gestation as embryonic nucleus.

2. Secondary Lens Fibers

Formed from equatorial cells of anterior epithelium throughout life. Concentric lamination pattern created. Named based on developmental timing:

  • Fetal nucleus: 3rd to 8th month
  • Infantile nucleus: Last week of fetal life to puberty
  • Adult nucleus: After puberty
  • Cortex: Superficial lens fibers of adult lens

3. Lens Capsule

True basement membrane developed from lens epithelium on its external aspect.

Features of Eye at Birth & Postnatal Development

At Birth

  1. Anteroposterior diameter: ~16.5 mm (70% of adult size; adult size reached by 7–8 years).
  2. Corneal diameter: ~10 mm (adult size 11.7 mm reached by 2 years).
  3. Anterior chamber: Shallow with narrow angle.
  4. Pupil: Small, does not dilate fully.
  5. Lens: Spherical; infantile nucleus present.
  6. Retina: Fully differentiated except macula (macula differentiates at 4–6 months postnatally).
  7. Optic nerve myelination: Reached lamina cribrosa.
  8. Refractive status: Hypermetropic by +2 to +3 D.
  9. Orbit: Underdeveloped; tear secretion absent.

Postnatal Period

  • Fixation develops in 1st month; completed by 6 months.
  • Macula fully developed by 4–6 months.
  • Fusional reflexes, stereopsis, and accommodation mature by 4–6 months.
  • Cornea reaches adult size by 2 years.
  • Lens grows continuously throughout life.

Physiology of Eye & Vision

Phototransduction

Initiation of vision in photoreceptors (rods and cones):

Incident Light → Structural change in photopigment → Activation of transducin → Activation of phosphodiesterase → Decreased intracellular cGMP → Closure of Na+ channels → Hyperpolarization → Decreased release of synaptic transmitter → Response in bipolar cells.

Visual Cycle [2000, 2003]

Rhodopsin consists of opsin coupled with 11-cis-retinal (max absorption ~500 nm). Light bleaches rhodopsin into all-trans-retinal + opsin. Regeneration occurs in dark/light equilibrium.

Rhodopsin Bleaching Cascade:
Rhodopsin → Bathorhodopsin → Lumirhodopsin → Metarhodopsin I → Metarhodopsin II → All-trans-retinal + Opsin.

Visual Perception Components

  1. Light Sense: Minimum brightness to evoke visual sensation. Tested after 20–30 mins dark adaptation.
    • Dark adaptation curve: Biphasic—initial small curve (cone adaptation) + secondary curve (rod adaptation). Delayed in Retinitis Pigmentosa and Vitamin A deficiency.
    • Light adaptation: Rapid process occurring within 5 minutes.
  2. Form Sense: Ability to discriminate shapes (cones at fovea). Measured via Snellen's chart.
  3. Sense of Contrast: Ability to perceive slight luminance differences. Measured using Pelli-Robson chart, Arden grating, etc.
  4. Color Sense: Mediated by cones (Young-Helmholtz Trichromatic Theory):
    • Red sensitive (Erythrolabe / LWS): Peak 565 nm
    • Green sensitive (Chlorolabe / MWS): Peak 535 nm
    • Blue sensitive (Cyanolabe / SWS): Peak 420–440 nm

High-Yield Review / One-Liners

  • Nasolacrimal duct opens into the inferior meatus; contains Valve of Hasner at lower end.
  • Orbital volume is 30 cc.
  • Thinnest wall of orbit: Medial wall. Thickest wall: Lateral wall.
  • Normal A:V ratio of retinal blood vessels is 2:3.
  • 'Safe zone' of eyeball is 8–9 mm behind limbus.
  • Yoke muscle for Right Superior Rectus is Left Inferior Oblique.
  • Short posterior ciliary arteries are 20 in number.
  • Muscle in lid attached to posterior tarsal margin with sympathetic innervation: Müller's muscle.
  • Most sensitive part of eye: Fovea centralis.
  • Volume of vitreous: 4 ml.
  • Only structure continuing growth throughout life: Lens.
  • Horner's muscle is the lacrimal part of Orbicularis oculi.
  • All visual reflexes are developed by 1 year.
  • Corneal endothelial cell count done by Specular microscopy.
